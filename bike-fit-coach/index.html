      How a Check Works

      A few minutes. The voice guides every step.

      1. Get ready. A chair and a box.
      2. Place the phone. Sideways, 4 big steps out.
      3. Level it. Follow the beeps.
      4. Get on. Sit as you ride.
      5. Hold. Near pedal at its lowest, still.
      6. Pedal. 20 seconds, or 30 on a trainer.
      7. One change. The first thing to fix.
      8. Check it now. The phone stays put.

      Where the Phone Goes

      Every Detail
      1. From the pedals, walk 4 big steps straight out, about 3 m. 2.5 to 3.5 m is fine.
      2. Stop within a hand's width of straight opposite the pedals.
      3. Chair back away from the bike, so it is never in the picture.
      4. The front edges of the seat and the box parallel to the bike.
      5. A box or books about 20 cm high on the seat.
      6. The phone sideways and upright at the box's front edge, the lens 60 to 80 cm up, about level with the tyre tops.
      7. Optional: both wheel centres the same distance from the phone, within a hand.
      8. Light behind the phone, never behind the bike.
      9. Nothing between the phone and your near leg and arm.
      10. Level it by the beeps, then leave it.

      What It Checks

      Your saddle height first, then your reach and bar height, on a road bike with drop handlebars. It cannot check a time trial or triathlon position on aero bars.

      Private by Design

      Everything stays on this phone. Your pain answer is never saved.

      How Exact It Is

      In one study of 26 cyclists filmed from the side, this kind of phone pose model was typically off by 3 to 9 degrees against lab markers, so small changes can hide inside that error. Good means more than 3 degrees inside the target. Fair means within 3 degrees of an edge, or a reading I cannot act on alone. It is a good start, not a professional fit. For pain or numbness that keeps coming back, see a professional bike fitter. Not medical advice.
